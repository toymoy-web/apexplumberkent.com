Why Does My Water Heater Smell Like Rotten Eggs? A Kent, WA Guide

It smells alarming, but a sulfur odor from hot water almost never means a gas leak. Here's what's actually happening inside the tank.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026 Updated October 2026

A rotten egg smell coming specifically from your hot water tap is one of the more alarming things a water heater can do, and the instinct to worry about a gas leak makes sense. In the large majority of cases, though, the smell has nothing to do with gas at all. It's a chemical reaction happening inside the tank itself, and it's usually a straightforward fix.

What's Actually Causing The Smell

Most water heaters have a sacrificial anode rod inside the tank, a rod of magnesium or aluminum that corrodes on purpose to protect the steel tank from rusting. Under certain water conditions, bacteria can react with that rod and produce hydrogen sulfide gas, which smells exactly like rotten eggs. This reaction happens inside the tank and has nothing to do with your gas line or gas supply, even on a gas-fired unit.

This is more likely to happen in water heaters that sit unused for a while, in tanks with certain water chemistry, or in units where the original anode rod has been in place for years without being checked. It's a real, common issue, not a sign that something has gone seriously wrong.

Signs It's The Anode Rod, Not A Real Gas Leak

Smell Only From Hot Water

If cold water doesn't smell but hot water does, that points to the tank itself, not a gas leak in the house.

No Smell Near The Gas Line Or Meter

A genuine gas leak usually has a noticeable smell right at the appliance connection or meter, not just from the tap.

Smell Is Stronger After The Tank Sits Unused

A day or two without running hot water often makes the smell more noticeable when you turn it back on.

Well Water Or Softened Water

Both can change the chemistry inside the tank in ways that make this reaction more likely.

How It's Actually Fixed

The fix is usually replacing the anode rod, sometimes with a different metal type (aluminum or zinc instead of magnesium) that's less likely to trigger the reaction in your specific water. In some cases, flushing the tank and disinfecting the system with a mild solution resolves it without a rod replacement at all.

This is exactly the kind of issue our water heater repair service handles directly, diagnosing the actual cause rather than defaulting to a full tank replacement for something that's usually a small parts fix.

If the smell keeps returning after a rod replacement, or the tank itself is old enough that other issues are showing up too, that's a different conversation. At that point a full water heater replacement genuinely might make more sense, especially if the unit is already past 10 to 12 years old. We'll tell you honestly which situation you're in rather than replacing a tank that just needs a part.

What To Do If You Smell Gas Near The Appliance

If you ever smell gas near the actual appliance, the gas line, or your meter, and not just from the hot water tap, that's a different situation entirely and should be treated as a real gas leak. Leave the area and contact your gas utility's emergency line immediately.

A rotten-egg smell that's isolated specifically to hot water at the tap is a water heater chemistry issue, not a gas emergency, but it's always worth knowing the difference. We've seen this come up on plenty of calls in older Covington homes, where a wave of original water heaters installed during the late-1990s building boom are now old enough for this exact issue to start showing up.

Smell Only At The Tap

If it's isolated to hot water at fixtures and nowhere near the appliance itself, treat it as a tank issue, not a gas emergency.

Smell Near The Appliance Or Meter

Leave the area immediately and call your gas utility's emergency line rather than investigating it yourself.

Don't Attempt To Relight A Gas Pilot Yourself

If you're unsure whether a smell is gas-related, let a technician assess it before relighting anything.

Ventilate The Area If Unsure

Opening a window near the water heater while you wait for an assessment is a reasonable precaution if you're not certain what you're smelling.

Does This Happen With Tankless Units Too?

Less often, but it's not impossible. Tankless water heaters don't store water in a tank the way traditional units do, so there's less standing water for the bacterial reaction to occur in, and most tankless systems don't use a sacrificial anode rod at all.

If you have a tankless unit and you're noticing a sulfur smell, the more likely cause is something in your home's water supply itself rather than the unit, and it's worth having your water tested to rule out a source further upstream.

That said, if your tankless system has an integrated buffer tank or a small internal reservoir, the same anode-related reaction can occur there on rare occasions. It's a much less common complaint on tankless calls than on standard tank calls, but it's not something we've never seen.

When To Just Replace The Anode Rod Yourself Versus Calling

A determined, comfortable DIYer can sometimes replace an anode rod with basic hand tools, but there are two real complications that trip people up. First, the rod is often torqued in tightly enough that it requires a breaker bar and significant leverage to remove, especially on a tank that's several years old.

Second, choosing the wrong replacement metal for your specific water chemistry means you're likely to be back at square one within a few months. For most homeowners, having it done as part of a professional visit is worth the cost just to get the right rod type the first time.

FAQs

Common Questions

Is a rotten egg smell from my water heater dangerous?

The smell itself isn't dangerous, though it's unpleasant. It's a sign of a chemical reaction inside the tank, not a gas leak, unless you also smell gas near the actual appliance or gas line.

Can I fix this myself?

Replacing an anode rod requires draining the tank and some specific tools, so most homeowners have this done professionally, especially since choosing the right replacement rod type matters.

Will this happen again after a fix?

Switching to a different anode rod material often resolves it permanently. If it keeps recurring, that's worth a deeper look at your specific water chemistry.

Should I call the City of Kent if my hot water smells?

Only if the cold water smells too. The City's water utility answers drinking water questions at 253-856-5600. If only the hot side smells, the cause is usually inside the water heater, and we can diagnose it.
