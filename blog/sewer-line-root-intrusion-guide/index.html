Sewer Line Root Intrusion: What Homeowners Should Know

Mature trees and older sewer lines are a genuinely common combination. Here's what root intrusion actually looks like before it becomes a full backup.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026

Tree roots don't grow toward a sewer line on purpose, looking specifically to cause damage. They grow toward moisture and nutrients, and a sewer line with even a hairline crack or a slightly separated joint is exactly the kind of moisture source roots are built to find. Once a root finds its way in, it doesn't stop. It keeps growing, and it brings more roots with it over time.

How It Actually Happens

Older clay and cast iron sewer pipe, common in homes built through the 1960s, has joints every few feet rather than the long, continuously sealed runs modern PVC pipe typically uses today. Those joints are the weak points. Ground settling, age, or minor shifting can open a small gap, and root hairs from a nearby tree are thin enough to find their way through even a very small opening. Once inside, the root has access to the exact moisture and organic material it wants, and it grows aggressively in that environment.

Signs You May Have Root Intrusion

Slow Drains Throughout The House

Not just one fixture, since a partial blockage in the main line affects everything upstream of it.

Gurgling From Toilets Or Drains

Air trapped behind a partial blockage often causes gurgling when other fixtures are used.

Recurring Backups In The Same Area

A backup that clears with cabling but returns within months is a strong sign of ongoing root growth, not a one-time clog.

Unusually Green Or Lush Grass Over The Line

Sewage leaking slightly from a root-damaged joint acts as fertilizer, and a noticeably greener patch of lawn over the line's path is a real, if unusual, warning sign.

Confirming It Before You Pay For A Fix

The only reliable way to confirm root intrusion, rather than guess based on symptoms, is a sewer camera inspection. The footage shows exactly where the roots have entered, how extensive the damage is, and whether it's isolated to one joint or spread across multiple sections of the line. This matters because the fix is different depending on severity, and a camera-confirmed diagnosis means you're not paying for more repair than the situation actually calls for.

What Actually Fixes It

For moderate root intrusion, hydro jetting clears the roots currently in the line and can buy meaningful time before they regrow, especially when paired with periodic maintenance jetting on a schedule. For more severe cases, where the pipe itself has cracked or separated significantly, a sewer line repair is the actual fix, sometimes using trenchless lining that repairs the damaged section without a full excavation. Any repair follows the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC) that Washington has adopted statewide. We've seen this exact pattern come up often on Maple Valley calls, where mature trees near older sewer laterals are a genuinely common combination.

One thing worth knowing honestly: as long as the tree causing the intrusion is still there, a repaired line can see new root growth again in the future. That's not a sign the repair failed. It's simply the ongoing reality of having a mature tree near a sewer line, and it's why some homeowners choose an occasional maintenance jetting schedule rather than treating one repair as a permanent solution.

Preventing It Where You Can

If you're planning new landscaping near a known sewer line path, choosing tree species with less aggressive root systems and planting them further from the line meaningfully reduces future risk. A root barrier, a physical barrier installed between a tree and the pipe, is another genuine prevention option for an existing tree that's too established to remove, redirecting root growth away from the line rather than eliminating the tree.

For a home with a known history of root problems, a periodic camera inspection, even without an active symptom, can catch new intrusion early, before it reaches the point of a slow drain or a backup. This is a reasonable step for a homeowner who's already dealt with root intrusion once and wants to stay ahead of it happening again, rather than waiting for the next set of symptoms to show up.

FAQs

Common Questions

Will removing the tree solve the problem?

It stops new root growth from that specific tree, though it doesn't repair damage roots have already caused. Removing a large, established tree also isn't always practical or desirable for other reasons.

How often should a line with known root intrusion be jetted?

Annually or every other year is common for a line with an ongoing root problem, though it depends on how aggressive the specific tree's growth is.

Can root intrusion cause a full sewage backup into my house?

Yes, if left unaddressed long enough. Catching it at the slow-drain stage is far less disruptive and less expensive than waiting for a full backup.

Is trenchless repair always an option for a root-damaged line?

Not always. A fully collapsed section or severe misalignment sometimes still requires traditional excavation, which is confirmed by the camera footage before any repair method is chosen.

How long does trenchless sewer line repair typically take?

Most trenchless repairs are completed in a single day, though curing time for the liner material can add a few extra hours to the full process.

Does homeowners insurance cover root-related sewer line damage?

Typically not, since root intrusion is considered gradual wear rather than sudden accidental damage. Coverage varies by policy, so check your specific terms.

Can I plant a new tree once a root problem is fixed?

Yes, but choose a species with a genuinely less aggressive root system, and plant it well away from the sewer line's known path to reduce future risk.

Suspect Roots In Your Sewer Line?

A camera inspection tells you for certain, before it becomes a backup.
