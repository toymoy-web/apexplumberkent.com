Is Bottled Water Better Than Kent, WA Tap Water? A Straight Answer

The honest answer isn't a simple yes or no, and it's not really about safety either way. Here's what actually differs.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026 Updated October 2026

This question comes up constantly, and most answers online lean hard in one direction or the other, either dismissing tap water entirely or insisting bottled water is a waste of money with no real upside. The honest answer is more specific than either extreme, and it depends more on taste preference and specific concerns than on any meaningful safety gap between the two.

The Safety Question, Answered Directly

Municipal water in the Kent area, like most public water systems in Washington, is required to meet federal and state safety standards, and it's regularly tested to confirm it does. Bottled water is also regulated, though under a different federal agency, and generally to a similar safety standard.

Neither option has a meaningful safety advantage over the other for a typical, healthy household. If you're choosing bottled water specifically because you're worried tap water isn't safe, that concern isn't well supported by the actual regulatory reality.

Where The Real Differences Actually Are

The genuine differences are taste, convenience, and environmental cost, not safety. Municipal water is treated with chlorine or chloramine for disinfection, and that treatment can leave a noticeable taste or smell that some people find unpleasant even though it isn't a health concern.

Bottled water, especially spring or purified varieties, often tastes different because it doesn't carry that same treatment profile. If taste is your actual objection to tap water, that's a real, valid preference, and it's also one that's solvable without buying bottled water indefinitely.

The Better Long-Term Fix, If Taste Is The Issue

A basic water filtration system, either a simple under-sink unit or a whole-home setup, addresses the chlorine taste directly at a fraction of the ongoing cost of bottled water, without the plastic waste. This is a genuinely common request we get, and it's usually the more sensible fix for a household that's buying bottled water primarily because of how the tap water tastes rather than any real safety concern.

If your concern is more about mineral content or general water hardness rather than taste specifically, that's a different, separate issue, and a water softener addresses hardness in a way that a basic filter doesn't. We've had this exact conversation with a number of SeaTac homeowners on older plumbing systems, where mineral buildup inside older pipes can sometimes affect both taste and pressure at the same time.

When Bottled Water Genuinely Makes More Sense

If you're on a private well rather than municipal water, the calculus changes, since well water isn't covered by municipal treatment or testing at all, and its quality depends entirely on your specific well and local groundwater conditions. In that situation, testing your actual water before assuming either bottled water or a filtration system is the right answer is the genuinely correct first step, rather than guessing.

What To Do Before Choosing Either Option

Identify The Actual Complaint

Taste, smell, hardness, and sediment each point toward a different fix. Naming the specific issue first avoids buying the wrong solution.

Check Whether You're On Municipal Water Or A Well

This single fact changes the entire recommendation, since well water isn't covered by municipal safety testing at all.

Test Well Water Before Assuming Anything

If you're on a well, get it tested rather than guessing based on taste or appearance alone.

Compare The Real Ongoing Cost

Weigh what you're currently spending on bottled water against a one-time filtration installation before deciding either way.

The Environmental And Cost Side, Honestly

A household that regularly buys bottled water for daily drinking and cooking is spending significantly more per gallon than municipal water costs, often by a wide margin. Over the course of a year, that adds up to a real, ongoing expense that a one-time filtration system installation typically pays back within a matter of months, depending on how much bottled water the household was buying before.

There's also the plastic waste to consider, which is a genuine environmental cost that a reusable filtration system simply doesn't carry. None of this means bottled water is unreasonable to buy occasionally or for specific situations, travel, an emergency supply, or a preference for convenience on a particular day. It's specifically the habit of buying it as a daily default, purely because of tap water taste, that a filtration system usually resolves more affordably.

A Note On Reverse Osmosis Systems

Some households consider a reverse osmosis system instead of basic carbon filtration, and it's worth knowing honestly where that fits. Reverse osmosis removes a broader range of dissolved substances than carbon filtration alone, at the cost of a slower flow rate, more waste water during filtration, and more frequent filter changes.

For most municipal water customers whose only real concern is chlorine taste, it's more system than the actual problem calls for, though it's a legitimate option for someone with a more specific water quality concern.

Local Note

What's In Kent's Tap Water, According To The City

The City of Kent's water quality report says its supply comes from 16 wells, 2 springs, and surface water purchased from Tacoma Water. It lists chlorination, fluoridation, and pH adjustment as the main treatment steps, with an average chlorine residual of 0.94 ppm and fluoride maintained at an average of 0.71 ppm.

It also lists 2023 hardness between 49 and 130 ppm, and the City answers water questions at 253-856-5600. A filter handles taste or chlorine and a softener handles hardness, so match the fix to what you notice. We also serve Des Moines and SeaTac.

FAQs

Common Questions

Is Kent's tap water actually safe to drink?

Yes, municipal water in the Kent area meets required state and federal safety standards. Filtration is a taste and preference choice, not a safety necessity.

Does a filter remove the chlorine taste completely?

A basic carbon filter, whether under-sink or whole-home, resolves the large majority of chlorine taste complaints effectively.

Should I test my water before choosing a system?

If you're on a private well, yes, definitely. If you're on municipal water and your main concern is taste, testing isn't usually necessary before choosing a basic filtration system.

How often does a basic filtration system need new filters?

It varies by system and household water use, but most basic carbon filters need replacement every few months to maintain full effectiveness.

Does filtered water taste noticeably different right away?

Yes, most households notice a difference in chlorine taste and odor immediately after a properly installed carbon filtration system is turned on.

Can a filtration system be installed on a rental property?

An under-sink system can generally be installed without permanent modifications, making it a reasonable option for renters, though it's worth checking with your landlord first.

Do I need a professional to install a basic under-sink filter?

A basic unit is sometimes a reasonable DIY project, though professional installation ensures it's connected correctly and won't create a leak at the connection point.

Where does Kent's tap water come from?

According to the City of Kent's water quality report, from 16 wells, 2 springs, and surface water purchased from Tacoma Water. The City also lists emergency interties with neighboring providers.
