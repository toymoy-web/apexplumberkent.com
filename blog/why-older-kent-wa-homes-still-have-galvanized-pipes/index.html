Why Older Kent, WA Homes Still Have Galvanized Pipes

It wasn't a mistake when it was installed. It's just reached the age where the material itself becomes the problem.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026 Updated October 2026

Galvanized steel pipe shows up constantly in homes built from the early 1900s through the 1960s, and for good reason. It was the standard, reliable choice for residential supply lines for most of the twentieth century, well before copper and modern plastics like PEX became the default.

If your home still has it, that's not a sign anything was done wrong when it was installed. It's simply a sign of the home's age catching up with a material that has a real, known lifespan.

Why It Was The Standard For So Long

Galvanized pipe is steel coated in zinc, which protects the steel from corrosion for a genuinely long time, often 40 to 60 years under normal conditions. That's a respectable service life for any building material, and it explains why builders used it so consistently for decades. The zinc coating does its job well while it lasts. The issue is what happens after it wears through.

What Happens As It Ages

Once the zinc coating degrades, the steel underneath starts corroding, and it does so from the inside of the pipe outward. This is part of why galvanized pipe failure can be hard to spot early.

The outside of an exposed pipe section in a basement can look fine while the inside diameter is narrowing with rust and mineral buildup. That internal narrowing is what causes the low water pressure and eventually the pinhole leaks that bring a lot of homeowners to us in the first place.

This pattern shows up regularly on calls from Des Moines, where a real share of the housing stock dates back to the years around the city's 1959 incorporation, right in the window where galvanized pipe was still the standard residential choice.

How To Tell If You Still Have It

Home Built Before The 1970s

Homes from this era are the most likely to still have original galvanized supply lines if they haven't already been repiped.

Magnetic Pipe

A magnet sticks to galvanized steel pipe. It won't stick to copper or PEX.

Dull Gray Color With Visible Threading

Galvanized pipe has a distinct gray color and threaded joint connections, different from copper's reddish tone.

Reduced Water Pressure Over Time

A gradual pressure decline across the whole house, not just one fixture, is a common sign of internal corrosion.

What This Actually Means For You

Having galvanized pipe doesn't mean you need to replace it tomorrow. Plenty of homes run on original galvanized lines for years without a serious issue, especially if the pipe is on the newer end of that era.

What it does mean is that the clock is running, and it's worth knowing where your home's pipe stands rather than being surprised by a leak. If you've already had more than one leak, or your pressure has genuinely dropped, that's usually the point where a full repipe starts making more financial sense than continuing to patch individual failures as they show up.

If you're not sure whether a specific leak is isolated or part of a bigger pattern, a proper leak detection assessment can help clarify that before you commit to a bigger repair. Sometimes what looks like a systemic pipe problem is actually a single failed fitting, and it's worth confirming which situation you're actually in before spending on a full repipe you might not need yet.

What A Real Assessment Actually Looks At

A legitimate assessment for galvanized pipe doesn't rely on guesswork. It involves checking accessible sections of pipe in a basement or crawlspace directly, testing water pressure at multiple fixtures throughout the house, and reviewing whether any past repairs are documented. A pipe that looks fine from the outside can still be significantly narrowed on the inside, which is why a visual check alone isn't enough to make a real recommendation either way.

For a home where only part of the system is galvanized, sometimes original steel supply lines mixed with newer copper or PEX from a partial past renovation, the assessment also needs to map out exactly which sections are which. This matters because a partial repipe focused on just the remaining galvanized sections is sometimes a genuinely reasonable middle option between doing nothing and repiping the entire house at once.

A Word On Timing

There's rarely a single moment where galvanized pipe suddenly becomes an emergency. The more common pattern is a gradual decline: slightly lower pressure this year, a pinhole leak next year, another one the year after. Homeowners who catch this pattern early and plan a repipe on their own timeline generally have a much better experience, and a lower total cost, than those who wait until a burst pipe forces an emergency decision on a compressed schedule.

Local Note

Galvanized Pipe In Kent: What A Water Report Can't Tell You

Kent's 2023 water quality report lists hardness between 49 and 130 ppm and names chlorination, fluoridation, and pH adjustment as its main treatment steps. Those numbers describe the water the City delivers. They do not describe what is happening inside a decades-old galvanized line in your wall.

Only a flow test, a pressure check, or a cut-out section of pipe shows that. If your faucets sputter or the water runs rust-colored, ask us for an assessment. We also serve nearby Des Moines and Tukwila.

FAQs

Common Questions

Is galvanized pipe dangerous to drink from?

Older galvanized pipe can leach some lead if the original fittings contained it, which was more common before the 1980s. If you're concerned, a water test can confirm whether this is an issue in your specific home.

How long does galvanized pipe actually last?

Typically 40 to 60 years, though it varies based on water chemistry and how the pipe was originally installed.

Can I replace just one section instead of the whole system?

You can, but it's worth knowing that connecting new pipe to old galvanized pipe can sometimes accelerate corrosion at the connection point. We'll walk you through the trade-offs for your specific situation.

Does homeowners insurance cover galvanized pipe failure?

Coverage varies significantly by policy. Sudden, accidental damage from a burst pipe is often covered, while gradual corrosion and wear typically are not. Check your specific policy for the details.

Can I judge my pipes from Kent's water quality report?

No. The report covers the water the City delivers, including hardness of 49 to 130 ppm in 2023, not the interior of your home's plumbing. Pipe condition takes an on-site assessment.
