Commercial Grease Trap Maintenance: What Local Restaurants Need to Know

Skipping grease trap maintenance doesn't just risk a backup. It risks a compliance violation too. Here's what's actually involved.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026

A grease trap is one of those pieces of restaurant infrastructure that only gets attention when something goes wrong, usually a backup during a busy service or a surprise question during a health inspection. Regular maintenance is far cheaper and far less disruptive than either of those situations, and it's worth understanding what the maintenance actually involves before you're dealing with an emergency.

What A Grease Trap Actually Does

A grease trap intercepts fats, oils, and grease from kitchen wastewater before it reaches the sewer line, allowing the lighter grease to float and separate from the water while solids settle to the bottom. Without it, that grease would travel into the sewer system, where it cools, solidifies, and causes blockages, both in your own line and potentially in the municipal system downstream. Grease interceptor sizing and installation requirements are covered under the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), which Washington has adopted statewide, and most jurisdictions require a functioning grease trap for any commercial kitchen for exactly this reason.

Why Skipping Maintenance Causes Real Problems

A grease trap that isn't pumped and cleaned on schedule eventually fills to capacity, at which point it stops doing its job and starts letting grease pass straight through into your sewer line. This is exactly how a restaurant ends up with a sudden, disruptive backup during service, usually at the worst possible time. It's also a real compliance issue. Many local jurisdictions inspect grease trap maintenance records, and a poorly maintained trap can result in fines or, in serious cases, a required shutdown until it's addressed.

What A Proper Maintenance Schedule Looks Like

Pumping Frequency Depends On Volume

A high-volume kitchen may need pumping monthly, while a smaller operation might go longer between services.

The 25% Rule

Many jurisdictions require pumping once grease and solids reach 25% of the trap's total capacity, not just when it's completely full.

Documentation Matters

Keeping service records is often a real compliance requirement, and it's useful to have on hand during a health inspection.

Between-Service Habits Help

Scraping plates before washing and avoiding pouring grease down drains extends the time between required pumpings.

What A Service Visit Actually Involves

A proper grease trap service starts with pumping out the accumulated grease and solids, followed by a thorough scraping and cleaning of the trap's interior surfaces, since grease can build up on the walls even after the bulk of it has been pumped out. The inlet and outlet baffles get checked too, since a damaged or missing baffle lets grease bypass the trap's separation process entirely, defeating the point of having one installed.

After service, we provide documentation of the visit, including the date and the volume removed, which becomes part of your compliance record. Keeping a consistent paper trail matters more than most kitchen operators realize until an inspector actually asks for it, and having it ready without scrambling is one less thing to worry about during an already stressful inspection day.

Interceptor Size And Kitchen Volume

Not every grease trap is sized correctly for the kitchen it serves, especially in a space that's changed hands or expanded its menu since the trap was originally installed. An undersized interceptor reaches capacity faster than expected regardless of how well you maintain a pumping schedule, which shows up as more frequent service needs than a comparable kitchen elsewhere. If your kitchen seems to need pumping unusually often, it's worth having the interceptor's sizing checked against your actual current volume rather than just accepting more frequent service as the new normal.

FAQs

Common Questions

How often does a grease trap actually need to be pumped?

It depends on your kitchen's volume, but many commercial kitchens need service monthly to quarterly. We can help establish the right schedule based on your specific operation.

What happens if I skip a scheduled pumping?

The trap can reach capacity and start passing grease through, leading to backups and potential compliance issues during an inspection.

Can you work around our business hours?

Yes, we regularly schedule commercial maintenance, including grease trap service, outside normal operating hours.

Do you provide documentation for health inspections?

Yes, every service visit includes documentation of the date and volume removed, which you can keep on hand for compliance records.

Can an undersized grease trap be replaced with a larger one?

Yes, and it's often the right call if a kitchen's volume has grown since the original trap was installed. We can assess your current sizing during a service visit.

Do grease trap maintenance requirements vary by jurisdiction?

Yes, exact pumping thresholds and inspection frequency can vary by local jurisdiction, so we confirm the specific requirements that apply to your location when setting up a schedule.

What happens during a health inspector's grease trap check?

An inspector typically reviews your maintenance records and may visually inspect the trap itself to confirm it's being serviced on the schedule your records show.
