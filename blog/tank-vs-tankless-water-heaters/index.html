Tank vs. Tankless Water Heaters: An Honest Comparison

Neither option is universally better. Here's an honest look at what actually differs, without the sales pitch either direction.

Written by the Kent Apex Plumbing team Published September 2026

Every water heater replacement conversation eventually gets to this question, and a lot of what's written about it online leans hard toward tankless being the obviously superior modern choice. The honest answer is more specific to your situation than that. Both options are genuinely good at what they do, and the right one depends on your household's hot water use, your home's existing setup, and how much you value upfront cost versus long-term efficiency.

How They Actually Differ

The lifespan and maintenance columns are worth sitting with for a second, because they change the real cost comparison. A tankless unit costs more upfront but can last close to twice as long, and that gap narrows the total cost of ownership significantly over 15 to 20 years, provided the annual maintenance actually happens.

FactorTank Water HeaterTankless Water Heater
Upfront costLowerHigher, often 2-3x
InstallationUsually straightforwardMay need new venting or gas capacity
Hot water supplyLimited by tank sizeContinuous, on demand
FootprintLarger, floor spaceSmaller, wall-mounted
Typical lifespan8-12 years15-20 years
MaintenanceOccasional flushAnnual flush required for warranty

When A Standard Tank Is Actually The Better Call

If you're replacing a failed tank with a similar unit and your household's hot water use is fairly typical, a standard tank replacement is usually the right, lower-cost answer. It's also the more straightforward install in a home that doesn't already have the venting or gas line capacity a tankless unit needs, since adding that capacity is a real added cost on top of the unit itself. A tank replacement handled properly, with correct sizing for your household, solves the problem without over-engineering it.

When Tankless Genuinely Makes Sense

Tankless makes the most sense for a household that regularly runs out of hot water with a tank system, values the smaller footprint, or is doing a fuller renovation where the plumbing and venting work is happening anyway. We've seen this trade-off come up most often on Black Diamond service calls, where a lot of the newer, higher-spec construction in that city already includes tankless systems from the start. Homeowners moving into that housing stock are choosing to keep and maintain what's there rather than switch.

One maintenance note that applies regardless of which system you choose: a tankless flush and descale is a condition of most manufacturer warranties, and skipping it is the single most common reason we see a tankless unit's performance decline over time. It's an easy, inexpensive step to forget precisely because a tankless unit doesn't fail all at once the way a leaking tank does.

Sizing Matters More Than The Tank-Vs-Tankless Decision Itself

A genuinely undersized unit of either type will disappoint you regardless of which technology you pick. For a tank system, that means matching the tank's gallon capacity to your household size and peak usage times, not just replacing a failed 40-gallon tank with another 40-gallon tank out of habit if your household has grown since it was installed. For tankless, sizing is measured in flow rate, gallons per minute the unit can heat on demand, and running two showers plus a dishwasher simultaneously requires a genuinely higher-capacity unit than a single-bathroom household needs.

This is where a generic online recommendation tends to fall short, since it can't account for your specific household's actual simultaneous hot water demand. An honest sizing conversation looks at how many bathrooms run at once during your household's busiest mornings, what appliances draw hot water, and whether you've had capacity complaints with your current system, not just the manufacturer's generic household-size chart.

The Question We Actually Ask Before Recommending Either One

Rather than starting from a technology preference, we start by asking what specifically is prompting the replacement. A failed tank that otherwise served the household fine usually points toward another tank, sized correctly, as the simplest and most cost-effective answer. A household that's outgrown its hot water capacity, or one already planning a larger renovation where the added installation work is a smaller marginal cost, is a better candidate for tankless.

We'll also factor in your home's existing gas line capacity and venting, since retrofitting a tankless unit into a home that wasn't built for one adds real cost that a straightforward tank swap doesn't carry. None of this is a sales pitch toward the more expensive option. A correctly sized tank system, properly maintained, serves most households perfectly well for its full service life, and there's no genuine efficiency argument that makes tankless the objectively correct choice for every home in every situation.

FAQs

Common Questions

Is tankless actually cheaper in the long run?

It can be, given the longer typical lifespan, but only if the annual maintenance is kept up. Skipping it shortens the lifespan advantage significantly.

Can I switch from tank to tankless without major work?

It depends on your home's existing gas line and venting. Some homes need upgrades to support a tankless unit, which adds to the install cost.

Which option has better resale value?

Tankless is often seen as a selling point in newer or renovated homes, though a properly sized, well-maintained tank system isn't a red flag to most buyers either.

How long does installation actually take for each option?

A straightforward tank swap is often completed same-day. A tankless install, especially one requiring new venting or gas line work, can take a full day or occasionally longer to finish properly.

Do both options qualify for any rebates?

Some heat pump tank models and certain high-efficiency tankless units qualify for utility rebates. Program details vary and change over time, so we'll point you to the current options when quoting.
